2.2
Verification, randomization and information acquisition
Estornell et al. minimize incentives through budgeted audits, including threshold and top-k settings
[4]. Zhang et al. study automated classification mechanisms with partial verification, including ran-
domized decisions and combinatorial costs [6]. Marginal-probability representations are established
in security-game optimization [3]. The finite oracle here specializes those tools to public report
classes and continuous quote recovery, with failed queries charged against capacity. Its complete
finite coverage is a certificate within the declared world universe, not a guarantee for unseen types.
Active feature acquisition addresses budgeted information selection [12]; its predictive objective
differs from the signed unilateral quotation difference audited here.
Recent acquisition methods include amortized greedy selection (GDFS) [17], discriminative in-
formation estimation (DIME) [18], stochastic latent encodings (SEFA) [19], and explanation-ranked
transformer policies [20]. Alongside ACO [12], they broaden acquisition coverage beyond simple
risk and predictive benefit. Their predictive objectives, sequential budgets and input assumptions
do not imply control of strategic quotation gain. Our executed adapters and their limitations are
specified in Appendix H.
2.3
Strategic feedback and contextual policy classes
Performative prediction analyzes retraining when deployment changes the data
[10]; strategic
empirical risk minimization differentiates through modeled user responses [11]. Contextual games
use public side information to condition decisions [7]. Joint score and audit design is also explicit
in recent audit-risk work [8]. We do not transfer their convergence conditions to our nonconvex,
finite-round training loop or introduce randomization as a new technique. Instead, we test an epoch-
refreshed implementation under matched fitting budgets and separately intervene on within-epoch
score refresh and training response-search strength. Our constructed policy-library separation also
includes a stronger small context-aware comparator that closes the gap. This prevents a restricted
library from being mistaken for evidence that dynamic weights or an LP solver are intrinsically
necessary.

Problem formulation
3.1
Reports, queries and seller utility
A batch contains n = 100 listings. Listing i has a latent recorded feature vector xi, including four
optional action fields: mileage, transmission, fuel type and body condition. Let Mi be its naturally
absent fields. A seller chooses a concealment set si containing at most two available action fields.
Its public report ri(si) replaces fields in Mi ∪si with missing values. Available values cannot be
fabricated, changed or recovered from public identifiers. A missingness indicator does not disclose
membership in Mi.
The platform observes the whole report batch r = (r1, . . . , rn), locks the reports and chooses
a possibly randomized allocation. It may query at most one publicly missing field per selected
listing, with at most B = ⌊bn⌋queries on every realized draw. A query of a concealed recorded
field recovers that value; a query of a naturally absent field fails. Both spend one unit. A fixed
itemwise model produces log quotes before and after the selected query. Scores and allocations at
deployment may use public reports and model outputs, but not the latent recoverability indicator
or the advertised target.
